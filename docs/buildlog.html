工 Project dossier  /  revision 0.1.0

Robot fighting
at Tekken 8 scale.

A ground-up 3D fighting game in Three.js. No imported assets — every texture, mesh, environment map and sound is generated in code, so the whole game ships as one self-contained file. This dossier tracks the decisions, the progress, and the screenshots as they come in.

The brief

What "AAA" has to mean here

The target is explicit: a fighting game that stands next to Tekken 8. That sets a bar on four axes at once — character rendering, animation, combat feel, and presentation — and a weakness in any one of them collapses the whole illusion. A beautifully lit robot that animates like a mannequin looks worse than a plain robot that moves with weight.

So the work is split across parallel workstreams with pinned APIs, and every visual subsystem is reviewed by a separate critic agent that scores captures against real Tekken 8 reference — blind, side by side. Anything that loses gets rebuilt.

Decisions on record

Choices that constrain everything downstream

D—01

Everything procedural

No glTF, no textures, no audio files, no webfonts. Meshes, PBR maps, the HDRI environment and every sound are synthesised at load.

WHY It keeps the game a single self-contained HTML file, and it removes the asset pipeline that would otherwise be the real bottleneck. The cost is that quality has to come from technique rather than from an artist's source files.
D—02

Fixed 60 Hz sim, free-running render

The simulation steps at exactly 1/60s and never varies. Rendering interpolates between the last two sim states.

WHY Fighting games are defined by frame data. If a jab is 10 frames of startup it must be 10 frames on every machine. Hitstop and slow-motion scale what enters the accumulator, never the tick itself.
D—03

Rigid plates, one bone each

The robots are skinned, but almost every armour plate is bound 100% to a single bone. Only cables and shrouds smooth-skin across joints.

WHY Smooth-skinning a mechanical character smears the panels at every joint and instantly reads as amateur. Rigid binding gives crisp hard-surface deformation, and the gaps it opens are exactly where the exposed pistons live.
D—04

Animation as authored data

Clips are hand-written keyframes — Euler degrees layered over the rest pose, times in ticks — not imported motion.

WHY It is diffable, reviewable and tunable by frame, which matters when a move's readability at its startup frame is a gameplay requirement and not an aesthetic preference.
D—05

Sim never talks to presentation

Combat emits events on a bus. Effects, audio, HUD and camera subscribe. The simulation has no reference to any of them.

WHY It keeps the sim deterministic and lets twelve workstreams build against one event contract instead of against each other.
D—06

Derived frame advantage

On-block and on-hit numbers are computed from stun minus recovery, never authored by hand.

WHY Published frame data that disagrees with what the engine actually does is the classic fighting-game bug. Deriving it makes the two impossible to desynchronise.

PHASE 00Foundation
rev 0.1.0

Contracts before code

Wrote the pieces every other workstream has to agree on before any of them could start in parallel: the 34-bone rig with its A-pose rest and IK chains, the clip format, the Tekken-style move schema with validation, the event bus, and the fixed timestep game shell.

Also pinned a clip-ID manifest so the animation and combat workstreams could reference each other's output without coordinating, and a static integrity checker that validates every clip against the skeleton and every move against the schema.

Fanned out the whole engine at once against the pinned charter — render pipeline and camera, procedural environment lighting, the material library, the robot mesh builder, roster and animator, two animation workstreams, combat, effects, arena, interface, and AI plus audio.

Each owns files nobody else touches, so they can run concurrently without collisions. Integration and the first build happen once they land.
